## 1. A Story to Start: A Yoga Guru Shakes Up the Shampoo Aisle (Patanjali)

* **The Old Guard**: For decades, giants like Hindustan Unilever dominated Indian shelves — soaps, shampoos, toothpaste — with few serious new challengers.
* **The New Entrant**: Patanjali, built around yoga guru Baba Ramdev's brand and an Ayurvedic, “swadeshi” positioning, entered FMCG and grew rapidly, pressuring established players on price and “natural” positioning.
* **The Lesson**: An industry that looked stable for decades could still be disrupted by a new entrant nobody in the boardroom had seriously modeled.

---

## 2. Core Concept: The Porter’s Five Forces of Market

* **Threat of New Entrants**: How easily can a new player join and compete?
* **Bargaining Power of Suppliers**: How much control a supplier has to control the supply of vital goods and services to a business?
* **Bargaining Power of Buyers**: How much control customers have to drive down prices, demand better quality, or request extra services.
* **Threat of Substitutes**: How easy it is for customers to switch to a different product or service.
* **Industry Rivalry**: How intense is the competition among existing firms in a market?

---

## 3. Force by Force: The Industry vs. Patanjali's Moves

### Force 1: Threat of New Entrants

* **Industry Reality**: Required distribution scale, working capital demands, and strong customer brand loyalty.
* **Patanjali's Dodge**:
  * *Not Kirana Shelves*: Built exclusive Arogya Kendras and Chikitsalayas.
  * *Not Brand Loyalty*: Leveraged national & spiritual trust around Baba Ramdev.

### Force 2: Threat of Substitute Products

* **Industry Reality**: Growing consumer skepticism about synthetic chemicals.
* **Patanjali’s Natural Stroke**: Positioned Ayurveda as a safe, natural, and non-toxic replacement, actively changing the market trend.

### Force 3: Industry Rivalry

* **Industry Reality**: Intense price wars and massive ad spends (10–15% of revenue).
* **How Patanjali Thrived**:
  * *Not Ads*: Relied on the stature and reach of Baba Ramdev.
  * *Swadeshi & Natural Claims*: Built a distinct, value-driven market segment.

### Force 4: Bargaining Power of Suppliers

* **Industry Reality**: Typically low bargaining power of agricultural suppliers in FMCG.
* **Patanjali Cutting Out Suppliers**: Achieved backward vertical integration by sourcing directly from farmers and building in-house processing.

### Force 5: Bargaining Power of Buyers

* **Industry Reality**: Price-sensitive consumers with low switching costs.
* **Patanjali’s Move**: Priced products 10–30% lower than competitors.

## 4. Back to Bonsho's Momos: Five Forces for a Momo Cart

* **Threat of New Entrants**: **HIGH** — Low startup costs and simple recipe requirements.
* **Industry Rivalry**: **HIGH** — Intense price competition and slim profit margins.
* **Supplier Bargaining Power**: **LOW** — Commodities available from countless competing local vendors.
* **Buyer Bargaining Power**: **HIGH** — Consumers face zero cost to switch to another shop for price and portion size.
* **Threat of Substitutes**: **HIGH** — Quick snacks like samosas, rolls, or burgers instead of momos.

---

## 5. Why You Must Understand Your Five Forces

1. A category with weak forces rewards even average execution; a category with strong forces punishes even brilliant execution.
2. Investors will ask about your competitive structure — not just your product.
3. Knowing your weakest force tells you exactly where to build your defence first.

## 7. SWOT Analysis

### A Story to Start: Boncho’s Momo Finally Writes Down Her Honest List

After a rough week — a competitor's cart opened nearby, and she ran out of onions twice — Priya sits down and honestly asks herself: what am I actually good at, and what's genuinely working against me? Fifteen minutes later, she has a clearer picture of her business than months of just showing up and cooking ever gave her. That exercise is **SWOT analysis**.

### Core Concept: What Is a SWOT Analysis?

* **Strengths**: What you're genuinely good at (inward).
* **Weaknesses**: Where you're genuinely vulnerable (inward).
* **Opportunities**: What's changing outside, in your favour (outward).
* **Threats**: What's changing outside, against you (outward).

### Looking Inward vs. Looking Outward

* **Looking Inward (Strengths & Weaknesses)**:
  * Live inside your business — things you can control, at least partly.
  * A strength is only useful if it's genuinely rare or hard to copy — “we work hard” isn't a real strength if every competitor also works hard.
  * A weakness is worth naming honestly, even when it's uncomfortable — the whole point is spotting it before a competitor or customer does.
* **Looking Outward (Opportunities & Threats)**:
  * Live outside your business — things happening in the world whether you act or not.
  * The same external event can be an opportunity for one business and a threat for another.

### SWOT vs. Five Forces

* **SWOT**: Looks at one specific business; mixes internal and external factors; quick, personal, easy to start with.
* **Five Forces**: Looks at the whole category's structure; purely external, structural pressures; more rigorous, built for comparing categories.

### Back to Priya's Momos: Her Full SWOT

* **Strengths**: Loyal regulars, fresh-ingredients reputation.
* **Weaknesses**: No backup plan when ingredients run out.
* **Opportunities**: A new hostel opening nearby next term.
* **Threats**: Two new delivery apps entering her area.

### Common SWOT Mistakes to Avoid

* Listing generic strengths every competitor could also claim (“good quality,” “great team”).
* Confusing an internal weakness with an external threat, or vice versa.
* Writing a long list instead of the few points that actually matter most.
* Doing it once and never updating it as the business or market changes.

## 8. Macro Environment: The PESTEL Framework

* Six macro-environmental forces bigger than any one industry — that shape what's possible for every business at once, whether they see it coming or not.

### The Six Factors (A. Nag, Ch. 6)

* **Political (P)**: Government decisions, policy shifts, and regulation — like a sudden currency policy change affecting every cash-based business overnight.
* **Economic (E)**: Inflation, interest rates, income growth — forces that change how much people can spend, and on what.
* **Social (S)**: Changing lifestyles, values, and habits — like a shift toward health-conscious eating or online shopping.
* **Technological (T)**: New tools that change what's possible — like UPI making digital payments instant and nearly free across India.
* **Environmental (E)**: Climate, sustainability pressure, and resource availability — like customers preferring less plastic packaging.
* **Legal (L)**: Laws and compliance requirements — like food safety licensing rules every street-food vendor must now follow.

## 9. Case Study: The Night India's Cash Disappeared (Demonetization — Nov 8, 2016)

### Lessons from Demonetization for Bonsho (Opening Jan 2017):

1. **Diversify Payment Channels** *(Technological & Economic)*: Never rely solely on cash.
2. **Maintain Operational Cash Reserves** *(Political & Economic)*: Buffer against liquidity shocks.
3. **Formalize Your Banking & Setup** *(Legal & Political)*: Ensure access to institutional credit.
4. **Build Strong Supply Chain Relationships** *(Economic & Social)*: Partner with suppliers who extend credit in crises.
5. **Establish Customer Loyalty over Transacting** *(Social)*: Loyal regulars return first.
6. **Manage Inventory Agility** *(Environmental & Economic)*: Keep lean inventory turns.

### Key PESTEL Insights:

* **Domino Effects**: How a single Political decision or Environmental rule instantly triggers Economic, Social, and Technological ripples.
* **Adaptability over Control**: Managers cannot control the PESTEL environment — they can only build business models agile enough to adapt to it.

## 11. Summary: Key Takeaways from Today

* **Five Forces**: Entrants, buyers, suppliers, substitutes, rivalry — together decide a category's real profitability. Substitutes are the force most often missed — watch it hardest.
* **Patanjali**: Proved that even decades-old, stable-looking industries can face a serious new entrant.
* **SWOT**: Four boxes (Strengths/Weaknesses inward, Opportunities/Threats outward). A real strength must be rare or hard to copy.
* **PESTEL**: Covers six macro forces (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal). Demonetisation shows how a sudden political decision can reshape an entire economy overnight.
* **Scenario Building**: Plan for several plausible futures, not just one prediction.

## 12. Term of the Day: Rasputitsa

* **Rasputitsa**: The seasonal mud periods that historically immobilized entire armies.
* In business: Macroeconomic, regulatory, or environmental conditions so severe that normal operations ground to a complete standstill.
