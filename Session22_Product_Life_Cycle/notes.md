# Week 6 Session 22: The Product Life Cycle — How Products Age

---

## 1. Core Concept: Products, Like Living Things, Age

* Every product exists on a timeline — and the strategy that works on day 100 will fail on day 1,000.
* **Theodore Levitt** introduced this framework in a classic **1965 Harvard Business Review article**: *“Exploit the Product Life Cycle.”*

$$\text{Introduction} \longrightarrow \text{Growth} \longrightarrow \text{Maturity} \longrightarrow \text{Decline}$$

| Stage | Market Dynamics | Primary Strategic Focus |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Introduction** | Product just launched. Sales are low, costs are high, and awareness is still building. | Build awareness, educate buyers, prove the category. |
| **2. Growth** | Sales accelerate fast. Competitors start noticing and entering the market. | Grab market share, expand distribution, out-execute rivals. |
| **3. Maturity** | Sales peak and flatten. The market is saturated; growth slows to near zero. | Defend share, protect margins, seek efficiency and extensions. |
| **4. Decline** | Sales fall as demand fades, tastes shift, or newer alternatives take over. | Harvest remaining cash, streamline, or exit cleanly. |

---

## 2. Real-World Case Studies by Stage

### 1. Introduction Stage — Ola Electric Scooters (Category Formation)
* **The Stage**: Ola launched its electric scooters into a market still forming, with low overall EV two-wheeler penetration in India.
* **What It Looks Like**: Heavy marketing spend, aggressive pricing to build trial, and a focus on awareness over profit.
* **Strategic Task**: Get early adopters on board and prove the category works, even at a loss — profit isn't the goal yet.

### 2. Growth Stage — UPI Payments (2018–2021)
* **The Stage**: Between roughly 2018 and 2021, UPI transaction volumes grew explosively as PhonePe, Google Pay, and Paytm all raced for share.
* **What It Looks Like**: Rapid adoption, new competitors entering monthly, and heavy feature-matching between rivals.
* **Strategic Task**: Grab share fast, because your growth-stage position often determines your maturity-stage position later.

### 3. Maturity Stage — Parle-G (Decades in Maturity)
* **The Stage**: Parle-G biscuits have held a stable, dominant position in India's glucose-biscuit category for decades.
* **What It Looks Like**: Sales are large but roughly flat year over year; competition is about defending share, not growing the category.
* **Strategic Task**: Protect margins, defend distribution, and look for small line extensions rather than expecting big new growth.

### 4. Decline Stage — Nokia Feature Phones (A Long, Slow Decline)
* **The Stage**: Once dominant, Nokia's classic feature phones lost relevance as smartphones took over the market after 2010.
* **What It Looks Like**: Falling sales, shrinking retailer interest, and shrinking investment in the product line.
* **Strategic Task**: Decide whether to milk remaining demand for cash, or exit the category entirely — Nokia largely exited.

## 3. Connecting the Dots: PLC and BCG, Side by Side

The two frameworks describe the same journey from different angles:

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Introduction} &\approx \text{Question Mark} \\
\text{Growth} &\approx \text{Star} \\
\text{Maturity} &\approx \text{Cash Cow} \\
\text{Decline} &\approx \text{Dog}
\end{aligned}$$

* **Key Insight**: The Product Life Cycle explains the **movement between BCG quadrants over time**.

## 4. Running Example: Boncho’s Momos on the PLC

* **Introduction**: Her new *fusion-momos menu*, launched just a few months ago.
* **Growth**: Her *catering-for-events line*, picking up fast word-of-mouth demand.
* **Maturity**: Her *original steamed momos* — steady, reliable, well-known locally.
* **Decline**: The *old snack item* she is phasing out due to falling demand.

## 5. Strategic Response: Extending the Life Cycle

* Maturity doesn't have to mean the end. Companies often extend a product's life through relaunches, new variants, or new uses.
* **Maggi Example**: Maggi has repeatedly added variants (*Atta Noodles, Cuppa Noodles, Special Masala*) to keep a decades-old product feeling fresh, rather than letting it slide into decline.

### Real-World PLC Variations and Patterns

| Pattern | Example | What Happened? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Introduction $\rightarrow$ Decline** | **Google Glass** | Launched with huge expectations but failed to gain mainstream consumer acceptance and quickly declined. |
| **Introduction $\rightarrow$ Growth (Rapid)** | **ChatGPT** | Adoption grew extraordinarily quickly after launch, with little conventional “slow introduction” phase. |
| **Growth $\rightarrow$ Decline** | **BlackBerry Smartphones** | Strong growth and maturity, followed by a rapid decline when iPhone/Android changed the market. |
| **Maturity $\rightarrow$ New Growth** | **LEGO** | Faced decline/near-crisis, then revived through new product lines, partnerships, and stronger customer engagement. |
| **Decline $\rightarrow$ Revival** | **Nokia 3310** | The original product declined, but the brand revived the product concept with a redesigned nostalgia version. |
| **Long Maturity** | **Coca-Cola** | Has remained a mature product for decades through continuous marketing, distribution, and product variations. |
| **Stage Skipped / Never Scaled** | **3D TV** | Introduced with considerable industry interest but never achieved mass-market growth and moved toward decline. |
| **Disrupted Before Maturity** | **DVD Players** | Digital streaming and smartphones accelerated decline before the product could enjoy a prolonged maturity period. |

## 6. Caution: Limitations and Common Pitfalls

### Limitations of the PLC
* Not every product follows a neat curve — some skip stages or jump straight to decline.
* It is easier to identify a stage in hindsight than to predict it in advance.
* Category-level cycles don't always match individual brand performance within that category.
* External shocks (a new technology, a pandemic) can override the natural curve entirely.

### Common Pitfalls
* Assuming maturity always means decline is coming soon — some products stay mature for decades.
* Applying growth-stage marketing spend to a product that's already mature.
* Ignoring how competitors' stage differs from your own, even in the same category.
* Forgetting that relaunches and line extensions can reset part of the curve.

## 7. Term of the Day: Madman Theory

* **Definition**: A strategic concept where a leader or entity makes adversaries believe that they are irrational, unpredictable, or volatile enough to take extreme actions, thereby forcing opponents to concede or negotiate out of fear of catastrophic escalation.
* **Historical Origin**: Associated with President Richard Nixon and Henry Kissinger during the Cold War / Vietnam War to pressure foreign adversaries into believing the US would use disproportionate force.
* **In Business**: Founders or aggressive competitors (e.g., in price wars or litigation) projecting an uncompromising, unpredictable persona willing to burn infinite capital to force rivals to back down or accept acquisition terms.

---

## 8. Summary: Key Takeaways from Today

* Every product moves through **Introduction, Growth, Maturity, and Decline**.
* Strategy needs to change at each stage — what works in Growth can fail in Maturity.
* **PLC and BCG describe the same journey**: stages roughly map to quadrants over time.
* Products can be extended past Maturity through relaunches, line extensions, and new use cases.
