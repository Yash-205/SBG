# PART 1: PORTER'S GENERIC COMPETITIVE STRATEGIES

---

## 1. Core Concept: Three Ways to Win, and Nothing In Between

* **Origin**: Introduced by **Michael Porter (1980)** in *Competitive Strategy*.
* A firm's relative position within its industry depends on whether its profitability is above or below the industry average.
* To achieve superior profitability, a firm must choose a clear generic strategy:

```
                            STRATEGIC ADVANTAGE
                     Low Cost             Uniqueness (Differentiation)
                 +----------------------+------------------------------+
  BROAD TARGET   |  1. Cost Leadership  |      2. Differentiation      |
STRATEGIC        +----------------------+------------------------------+
  SCOPE          |  3A. Cost Focus      |      3B. Differentiation     |
  NARROW TARGET  |                      |          Focus               |
                 +----------------------+------------------------------+
```

| Strategy | Definition | Core Mechanism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Cost Leadership** | Be the lowest-cost producer in the industry, serving a broad market. | Relentless cost control, economies of scale, standard products, lean supply chain. |
| **2. Differentiation** | Be genuinely unique on dimensions widely valued by customers, broad market. | Premium quality, distinct brand prestige, superior customer experience, proprietary technology. |
| **3. Focus** | Serve one narrow segment (niche) better than any generalist can. | Tailored specifically to the niche's exact needs: via **Cost Focus** or **Differentiation Focus**. |

---

## 2. The Danger Zone: Stuck in the Middle

* **Definition**: A company that attempts to pursue all strategies simultaneously without mastering any ends up **“stuck in the middle.”**
* It lacks the market share and low costs to compete with cost leaders, and lacks the uniqueness or brand loyalty to compete with differentiators.
* **The Jet Airways Case Study**:
  * Attempted to offer full-service luxury while competing on aggressive discount pricing against low-cost carriers like IndiGo.
  * Lost price-sensitive travelers to IndiGo and premium travelers to more consistent legacy/international airlines — leading to financial collapse.

---

## 3. Real-World Indian Case Studies

### 1. Cost Leadership — IndiGo Airlines
* **Strategy**: Standardized single-family fleet (Airbus A320), quick 25-minute aircraft turnarounds, unbundled services, point-to-point routes.
* **Result**: Lowest cost per available seat-kilometer (ASK), enabling profitable fares that rivals cannot match without losing money.

### 2. Differentiation — Titan (Tanishq) / Royal Enfield
* **Strategy**: Tanishq transformed unorganized Indian jewelry buying through caratmeters, certified purity, transparent pricing, and design trust.
* **Result**: Commands a sustainable premium price because customers value the guaranteed trust and emotional experience.

### 3. Focus — Fabindia / Forest Essentials
* **Strategy**: Tailored specifically to consumers seeking authentic traditional craft, handlooms, and Ayurvedic luxury.
* **Result**: Dominates a well-defined niche rather than trying to serve the entire mass consumer market.

---

## 4. A Common Mistake: Confusing Low Price with Cost Leadership

* Discounting is easy to copy. A rival can match your price tomorrow.
* **Real Cost Leadership** means your rival literally cannot match your price without losing money, because your cost structure is genuinely, structurally lower.
* If a rival can copy your price without pain, you don't have a cost leadership strategy — you have a temporary promotion.

---

## 5. From the Art of War: Sun Tzu

> *“Do Not Repeat the Tactics Which Have Gained You One Victory”*

* Sun Tzu warned against mechanically repeating a winning tactic, because circumstances change and rivals adapt to what worked before.
* A generic strategy is not a tactic to copy once — it's a discipline to sustain. Cost leaders must keep finding new efficiencies; differentiators must keep the gap from closing as rivals catch up.

# PART 2: BLUE OCEAN STRATEGY VS. RED OCEAN STRATEGY

---

## 6. Core Concept: Red Ocean vs. Blue Ocean

* **Origin**: Developed by **W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne (2005)** in *Blue Ocean Strategy*.

| Dimension | Red Ocean Strategy | Blue Ocean Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Market Space** | Compete in existing market space. | Create uncontested market space. |
| **Competition** | Beat the competition. | Make the competition irrelevant. |
| **Demand** | Exploit existing demand. | Create and capture new demand. |
| **Trade-Off** | Make the value-cost trade-off. | **Break the value-cost trade-off**. |
| **Strategic Alignment** | Align activities around low cost **OR** differentiation. | Align activities around **BOTH low cost AND differentiation**. |

---

## 7. The Central Idea: Breaking the Cost vs. Value Trade-Off

* Porter's world assumes you must choose: low cost, or high value, rarely both.
* **Blue Ocean Strategy** argues that real breakthroughs happen when a company pursues both simultaneously, by fundamentally rethinking what the industry even competes on.
* *Not “how do we win the current game,” but “what if the game itself changed?”*

---

## 8. The Canonical Case Story: Cirque du Soleil (1984, Quebec)

* **The Red Ocean**: Traditional circus industry was shrinking — animal-rights backlash, high star-performer costs, and fierce competition fighting over a declining children's audience.
* **The Blue Ocean Move**: Eliminated animal acts and star performers entirely (cutting massive costs), while adding theatrical narrative, original musical score, and artistic sophistication.
* **Result**: Competed with neither traditional circuses nor Broadway theatre — it created an entirely new category for adult, high-paying audiences.

---

## 9. Core Tool: The Four Actions Framework (ERRC Grid)

To break the trade-off between differentiation and low cost, ask four fundamental questions:

| Action | Core Question | Cirque du Soleil Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ELIMINATE** | Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be removed entirely? | Star performers, animal acts, aisle concession sales. |
| **REDUCE** | Which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard? | Fun and humour (toned down from slapstick). |
| **RAISE** | Which factors should be raised well above the industry standard? | Unique venue experience (climate-controlled, comfortable seating). |
| **CREATE** | Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered? | Artistic themes, refined viewing environment, live original music score. |

---

## 10. Real-World Indian Case Study: Practo

* **The Problem Before Practo**: Finding a doctor meant word-of-mouth uncertainty or unverified directories; booking required endless phone calls or physical queues.
* **The Four Actions Applied**:
  * **Eliminated**: Uncertainty of word-of-mouth referrals.
  * **Reduced**: Friction of phone booking and waiting room queues.
  * **Raised**: Transparency through verified patient reviews and instant appointment slots.
  * **Created**: Online teleconsultation and cloud digital health records.
* **Result**: Did not compete directly with hospitals or directories — built the uncontested space between them.

---

## 11. Core Tool: The Strategy Canvas

* A simple diagnostic and action chart:
  * **Horizontal Axis**: List the key factors the industry competes on and invests in.
  * **Vertical Axis**: Plot the offering level (low to high) that buyers receive.
* **Red Ocean Curve**: Most rivals' lines look almost identical — that sameness *is* the Red Ocean.
* **Blue Ocean Curve**: Produces a visibly different curve — high on factors rivals ignore, low or zero on factors rivals obsess over.

---

## 12. A Common Mistake: Not Every New Idea Is a Blue Ocean

* A genuine blue ocean survives the Four Actions test on all four dimensions and stays defensible.
* Most “we're totally different” startup pitches are really a conventional differentiation strategy inside an existing Red Ocean, dressed up in bolder marketing.

---

## 13. Running Example: Could Boncho’s Momos Find a Blue Ocean?

| Action | Strategic Application for Boncho's Momos |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Eliminate** | The physical queue entirely — pre-order only with guaranteed pickup time slots. |
| **Reduce** | Menu complexity — a tight 4-item signature menu instead of 15 mediocre items. |
| **Raise** | Ingredient transparency — a visible farm-to-plate sourcing story customers can see. |
| **Create** | A subscription model — a weekly momo box for college hostel students who order weekly anyway. |

---

## 14. Connecting Back: Blue Ocean Doesn't Replace Earlier Tools

* A genuine Blue Ocean is rare, and even after finding one, the tools from earlier weeks still apply.
* You still need a real situation analysis, a real customer segment, and eventually, a defensible position once rivals inevitably notice and copy.
* **Rule**: Blue Ocean Strategy is a way to find *where* to compete. Everything else you've learned tells you how to *defend* it once you are there.

---

## 15. Key Takeaways from Today

* **Porter's Three Generic Strategies**: Cost Leadership, Differentiation, and Focus — trying to do all three usually means winning at none (*Stuck in the Middle*).
* **Cost Leadership** is a structural cost advantage rivals cannot match without pain, not a temporary discount.
* **Red Oceans** compete in existing market space with known rivals; **Blue Oceans** create uncontested market space with new demand.
* The **Four Actions Framework (Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create)** is the operational engine for breaking the value-cost trade-off.
